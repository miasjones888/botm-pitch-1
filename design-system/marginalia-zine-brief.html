Marginalia · zine — design brief and wireframes.

Production brief for the printed essay-object that ships in the BOTM box. Built around restraint as the operating principle — single-image-per-page chapbook, white space as compositional element, type set with care. Extends the locked Marginalia design system v2. To be handed to Claude Design or used as the working document for an in-Canva build. Not a finished mockup.

format A6 closed (~4×6") · folded chapbook open spread ~8×6" spreads 6 quiet + 2 density (8 total available) register small literary monograph operating principle restraint, with earned exceptions
01 · principles

What this object is, and what it isn't.

core constraints

Five rules that hold the publication together. Every page must answer to them. If any layout decision violates one of these, the decision is wrong.

i.
White space is the design.

Each page should feel as though most of it is intentionally empty. The cream paper is not background — it is composition. Generous margins on every edge. If a page feels full, something must come off it.

ii.
One precious thing per page.

Each page holds one image, or one block of type, or one quiet typographic moment — and then it stops. Pages are not collections. The thing on the page is the page. A second element only earns its place if it is small, supporting, and obviously secondary.

iii.
Real photography. Treated. Small.

When images appear, they are real (a pressed plant, a window, a hand on a book) and they are treated (halftone, duotone, scan-edged). They are placed at modest scale within a wide cream field. They never bleed to the edge. They never overlap each other. They are not collaged. They are placed.

iv.
Type with care.

Body text in modest sans, set in narrow measure, generous leading. Captions in small mono, set with conviction. One display moment per spread at most — and only when the spread truly needs one. Italic is used sparingly and always means something. Spacing and scale carry the meaning, not weight or ornament.

v.
Hand element used once. Across the whole publication.

There is exactly one handwritten moment in the entire object — Mia's signature on the colophon. That is the only Reenie Beanie. No marginalia annotations, no scattered hand notes, no curved arrows. The publication is named Marginalia; it does not need to perform marginalia. The handwriting is reserved for the closing signature, where it lands.

vi.
Density when it earns its place.

Up to two spreads in the publication may break rules i. and ii. — they may be visually denser, with multiple elements organized as constellation, catalog, or index. These spreads earn their density by doing work the quiet spreads cannot — showing many things in relation, mapping connections, indexing a chain of references. Density is not decoration. If a dense spread can't justify why it is busy, it should be quiet.

02 · materials

The vocabulary, kept small.

treatment swatches

Ten treatments — fewer than before, intentionally. Each swatch shows its element at its actual relative scale within a cream field. Notice how much cream sits around each element. That ratio is the design.

m.01 · primary
Pressed plant scan

A single botanical specimen scanned flat against cream. Modest scale — occupies roughly 25–35% of the page area, surrounded by generous cream margin. The image is centered or slightly offset, never bleeding. Reference: Echter Arznei-Baldrian, herbarium plates, Carson's Nox botanicals.

m.02 · primary
Single treated photograph

A photograph treated to halftone or duotone (forest green, navy, or ink). Placed at modest scale on cream. A single element. No tape, no rotation, no overlap. Reference: Beuys in Kleve photo trio, "iterations" landscape thumbnails, the Lots Sheppard duotone landscapes.

m.03
Duotone landscape

A landscape photograph reduced to two tones — typically navy + cream, or forest green + cream. Used once across the publication, at slightly larger scale than m.02, when an opening or closing breath is called for. Reference: ARC'TERYX hill, the green forest spread, mental health awareness 2023 landscape.

m.04
Single image plate

A single contained image with thin border, treated. The most architectural use of an image — when the page wants to feel like a museum plate. Generous cream around all four sides. Reference: Earth Era cover, the Sebald-style photographic plates with captions.

m.05
Italic body text in narrow measure

Sans italic, set narrow, generous leading, sitting in a wide cream field. Used for quoted passages, observations, voice-of-the-reader fragments. The text is typically 30–40% of the page width, surrounded by margin. Reference: literary chapbook spreads, the Earth Era body text.

m.06
Display moment

A single short phrase set at display scale — large sans, tight letter spacing, dropped into a sea of cream. Used at most once per spread, often only once or twice across the whole publication. The page's quiet declaration. Reference: "less is more or less," "the tongue is an eye," chapbook openers.

m.07
Mono caption / metadata

Small uppercase mono with wide letter spacing. Plate numbers, captions, folio marks, dates. Sits low and small, never competing with primary content. The connective tissue of the object. Reference: every literary monograph caption convention.

m.08 · once only
Handwritten signature

Reenie Beanie, navy, slight rotation. Used exactly once across the publication — Mia's signature on the colophon. Not a marginalia annotation, not a scattered note, not a margin arrow. One moment of hand, where it belongs. Reference: a real signed letter, a real handwritten sign-off.

m.09 · the lavender
Edition stamp

Small lavender-pale rectangle with thin border and "M." monogram inside. Slight tilt. Used twice in the publication — once on the cover, once on the colophon. The lavender moment. Never appears on interior spreads. Reference: postal artifacts, edition marks on small-press publications, the deck's closing-slide postage stamp.

m.10 · base
Paper texture

Subtle SVG noise at low opacity, multiply blend. Applied as a base layer to every spread. Suggests uncoated stock under print. Always present, never noticed. Carries the warmth of real paper through a digital surface.

m.11 · density
Image constellation

Multiple small treated images placed sparsely on a cream field, with implied geometry between them — sometimes thin connecting lines, sometimes just spatial relationships. Reads as constellation chart or archaeological diagram. Used on density spreads only. Reference: "signs from the past" plate, the marble "essence of the ordinary" composition.

m.12 · density
Catalogued grid

A strict grid of small treated images (typically 3×3 or 4×3), each with a small mono caption beneath identifying it. Reads as plate index from a natural-history monograph or as a personal catalog of the reading. Used on density spreads only. Reference: Polaroid contact sheet with handwritten labels, the table-of-contents object inventory.

m.13 · density
Connecting line

A thin red-brown line drawn between two small images, suggesting connection or chronological link. Used only on the constellation spread to draw the eye along an implied chain. Color is a single muted red-brown — not navy, not lavender, not ink. The line is doing diagrammatic work, not decoration. Reference: archaeological plate diagrams, scholarly notation lines.

06 · handoff

For Claude Design or in-Canva build.

execution notes

Practical instructions for whoever picks this up next.

a.
Start by counting elements.

For each spread, list the elements before placing them. If the count exceeds the wireframe's element count, something is wrong. The number of elements per spread is itself a design constraint.

b.
Source images first. Treat before placing.

Gather or shoot real photographic content (pressed plant scan, treated landscape, single object). Apply halftone or duotone treatment in Photoshop or via Canva effects before placing into layout. Untreated photography reads wrong even on a right layout.

c.
Treat the margin as sacred.

The dashed border on each wireframe is the safe-zone — content lives inside it, white space lives outside it. Do not push elements into the margin. The breathing room is not negotiable.

d.
Lock copy before laying out.

Use the copy register guide to draft each slot's writing before opening Canva. Layout against finished copy is faster and produces better composition than layout against placeholder Lorem ipsum.

e.
Build cover last.

The cover is the simplest spread but should reflect the issue's interior. Build interior spreads first; let the cover absorb their tone. The closing line on the colophon must match the deck's Slide 05 verbatim — this is the link between formats.

f.
If a page feels full, remove something.

When in doubt, take an element off. The publication's strength comes from what it withholds. The reader fills the cream space with their own attention — which is the only kind of attention worth having.
