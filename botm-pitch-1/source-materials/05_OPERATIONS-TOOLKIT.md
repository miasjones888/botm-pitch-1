# OPERATIONS TOOLKIT — How Found Text Becomes Your Own
*The inventory of moves. Read once before Phase 4. Reread when you're stuck.*

---

## THE PRINCIPLE

The source's literary charge is the fuel, not the obstacle. What makes the work yours is **operations performed on the source** — not finding source material blank enough to write into.

This artifact catalogs the operations available to you. They are not exhaustive. They are not exclusive. Most published hybrid-genre work uses multiple operations layered. The point is to make the moves *visible* so you can choose them deliberately rather than defaulting to "quote and arrange."

The bar is: **the resulting work has to do something the source couldn't do alone.** Transform, not transcribe.

---

## TIER 1 — STRUCTURAL OPERATIONS
*Operations that change what the source IS, not just what it says.*

### 1. Erasure

**Move:** Remove most of the source text. Leave words/phrases in their original positions. The negative space is part of the form.

**What it does:** Forces the source to say what it didn't say. Reveals what was already there. The act of cutting *is* the writing.

**Tradition:** Mary Ruefle, *A Little White Shadow* (erases a Victorian novel). Srikanth Reddy, *Voyager* (erases Kurt Waldheim's UN secretary memoir — devastating use because Waldheim was a Nazi). Jen Bervin, *Nets* (erases Shakespeare's sonnets). Tom Phillips, *A Humument* (erases a Victorian novel for 50 years).

**Applied to your source:** You could erase the medical log into a long poem about absence and waiting. Erase the family letter into an unsignable apology. Erase the caretaker notes into a list of what gets touched in a day. The reader sees the original page (or its trace) with most of it grayed out or struck through.

**Production:** Visually demanding. Works extremely well in your monospace/Direction-B aesthetic. Each erased page is its own composition.

**When to use:** When you want to do the most radical thing with the source AND have the most contained scope. Erasure is small. It is finishable. It is also the form most likely to produce a piece that reads as *unmistakably yours* despite using only the source's words.

---

### 2. Re-sequencing

**Move:** Take the source's content out of chronological/original order and reorder by some other logic — image, theme, emotional register, length, repetition, alphabetical, reverse chronological.

**What it does:** Breaks the source's claim to be a record. Asserts authorial logic on top of the source's. Reveals patterns the chronological order hid.

**Tradition:** Heaps of memoir and lyric essay. Carson reorders constantly. *Bluets* is sequenced by number, not chronology. Sebald's prose juxtaposes images and text in self-determined order.

**Applied to your source:** All vital sign blocks together, in sequence. All times the father said *Where's Jay?* in order. All references to the mother. All references to the phone. All times someone left the room. All times a stranger did the work of family.

**When to use:** When the source has hidden patterns you want to surface. Often combined with annotation or selection.

---

### 3. Fragmentation

**Move:** Break the source into smaller units than it presents itself in. Sentences become lines. Paragraphs become fragments. Days become moments. Then those small units become the working scale.

**What it does:** Slows reading down to the speed of attention. Forces each unit to bear more weight than the original syntax intended. Lyric pressure.

**Tradition:** Renee Gladman, *Calamities*. Carson, almost everywhere. Bhanu Kapil, *Ban en Banlieue*. Sarah Manguso, *300 Arguments*.

**Applied to your source:** A page that is one sentence pulled from the document, with white space around it. The MRI line on its own page. *Feels like Heaven* on its own page. *He was completely naked waste-down in the laundry room.*

**When to use:** When you want the source's flat clinical voice to land with poetic force. Often the simplest move and the most powerful.

---

### 4. Erasure-by-replacement

**Move:** Like erasure, but instead of removing words, you *replace* them with other words (often your own). The source's sentence shape is preserved; the content shifts.

**What it does:** Creates an uncanny doppelgänger of the source. The reader feels the original behind the rewrite.

**Tradition:** Kenneth Goldsmith, conceptual writing in general. Jen Bervin's *Nets* uses bolding instead of replacement, but the principle is similar. Charles Reznikoff, *Testimony* (court records as poetry, with selective rewording).

**Applied to your source:** You take the family letter and rewrite it, keeping the structure ("Dear Family, We wanted to update you on...") but replacing what's being updated. You take a vital sign block and replace the numbers with something else (emotions on a 0-100 scale, distances from home, hours since last spoken to your father).

**When to use:** Sparingly. One or two instances in a piece. Powerful as a single late-stage move; tedious as a whole strategy.

---

### 5. Annotation

**Move:** Treat the source as the primary text and write your voice as marginalia, footnotes, commentary, gloss, glossary, or critical apparatus around it.

**What it does:** Makes you the scholar/reader/witness of the source rather than its reproducer. The reader sees the source THROUGH your attention to it.

**Tradition:** Anne Carson, *Nox* (the entire book is an annotated translation of one Catullus poem about her brother). Susan Howe's archival work. *House of Leaves* (Mark Z. Danielewski). Jenny Boully, *The Body: An Essay* (which is entirely footnotes — the source text never appears).

**Applied to your source:** The document is reproduced (in part). You write commentary in the margins, footnotes at the bottom, asterisks pulling out detail. Your voice doesn't *replace* the document — it surrounds it.

**Note:** This is what `03_DEEP-ANNOTATION.md` is. You could literally take that artifact, rewrite it in your voice (mine is too much, too professorial), and that becomes large portions of the piece. The annotation already exists as scaffold.

**When to use:** When you want the document foregrounded but want your sensibility on top of it. The "Implied" presence option in Phase 4 routes here naturally.

---

### 6. Indexing / Glossary

**Move:** Make an index, glossary, key, or table of contents to the source — but the entries are written by you.

**What it does:** Treats the source as a database. Asserts that this document is reference material for *your* reading of it.

**Tradition:** Carson, *Autobiography of Red* (has a "fragment" structure that functions like an index). Borges. Calvino. The entire genre of conceptual writing.

**Applied to your source:** *Index of falls. Index of devices that failed. Index of food not eaten. Index of times someone said "Where's Jay?" Glossary of medical terms that mean dying. Key to the family's emotional shorthand.* Each entry could be one line, or could expand into a paragraph.

**When to use:** When the source is too dense to address whole-cloth and you want the formal logic of a reference work doing the structural labor.

---

### 7. Translation (without translating)

**Move:** Treat the source as if it were in another language and "translate" it into a different register, tone, or voice.

**What it does:** Makes voice itself the subject. Reveals what register obscures.

**Tradition:** Caroline Bergvall, *Drift* (translates Old English). David Cameron, *Flowers of Bad* (translates Baudelaire's *Les Fleurs du Mal* through deliberate mistranslation). Anne Carson translates Sappho with words intact and fragments left as fragments.

**Applied to your source:** Translate the medical log into the language of the body. Translate the family letter into the language of children's books. Translate the caretaker's notes into the language of liturgy. Translate the discovery scene into a real estate listing.

**When to use:** Conceptually risky, formally striking. Best as one section of a larger piece, not the whole.

## TIER 2 — JUXTAPOSITION OPERATIONS
*Operations that gain force from what they put NEXT TO the source.*

### 8. Voice-braiding

**Move:** Alternate the source's voice with your own voice in distinct passages. The reader moves between two registers.

**What it does:** Creates dialogue without conversation. The two voices comment on each other through proximity.

**Tradition:** *Don't Let Me Be Lonely* (Rankine's voice + TV news + pharmaceutical inserts). *Bluets* in some passages. Jenny Offill, *Dept. of Speculation* (multiple voices, multiple sources). *Ban en Banlieue.*

**Applied to your source:** Document → your voice → document → your voice. Could be: one timestamped log entry → one fragment of yours → one timestamped log entry → one fragment. Could be: large blocks alternating chapter-style. Could be: micro-juxtapositions on a single page.

**Note:** This is what most readers picture when they hear "hybrid genre." It's the most familiar move. Which makes it both the most legible and the most worn.

**When to use:** When you have actual material from your own life you're willing to put on the page. Requires the *Explicit* presence option in Phase 4.

---

### 9. Image juxtaposition

**Move:** Pair text from the source with images (yours or found) that are not illustrations of the text.

**What it does:** The reader makes meaning across the gap. Image and text neither agree nor disagree — they vibrate.

**Tradition:** W.G. Sebald, *Austerlitz* and *The Rings of Saturn* (uncaptioned photos throughout). Roland Barthes, *Camera Lucida*. Carmen Maria Machado, *In the Dream House* (less imagistic but uses formal apparatus similarly).

**Applied to your source:** *Feels like Heaven* + a photograph of a hospital ceiling. The vital sign block + a photograph of an empty couch. The discovery scene + a photograph of a mail slot. None of these illustrate. They sit with.

**Production note:** Per `04_AESTHETIC-AND-ZINE-DIRECTION.md`, almost no photographic imagery is recommended for v1. If you want to use this operation, the images should be 1-bit, ASCII, or diagrammatic — not photographic.

**When to use:** When text alone feels too thin and you want the page to hold material attention.

---

### 10. Apparatus-juxtaposition

**Move:** Pair the source with the formal apparatus of an unrelated document — a legal contract, a property listing, a recipe, a children's book, a service manual, an autopsy report, a will.

**What it does:** Genre collision. Forces a tonal third space.

**Tradition:** Heidi Julavits, *The Folded Clock* (diary form mimicked deliberately). Maggie Nelson, *The Argonauts* (theory braided with memoir). Bhanu Kapil's recurring use of police-form structure.

**Applied to your source:** The discovery scene formatted as a real estate listing. The family letter formatted as a final will. The caretaker notes formatted as a Yelp review. The hospitalization formatted as an Amazon return form.

**When to use:** Requires real risk-tolerance. Can read as gimmicky if not earned. But when it lands, it lands hard. Best done once or twice in a piece.

---

### 11. Time-collapse

**Move:** Place material from radically different times next to each other without transition. The source's October 2024 next to your 1996. Your grandfather's death two years ago next to the document's father's death.

**What it does:** Asserts that the past is a present condition. Refuses linear time. Makes the source legible as not just *about* dying but *as* a structure of dying that recurs.

**Tradition:** Carson, almost always. Sebald. *In the Dream House.* The whole tradition of haunted memoir.

**Applied to your source:** Hospital, 2024. Then a fragment of you in your mother's hospital room, 2024 also. Then your grandfather's hospital, 2023. Then the document's father falling, 2024. The reader has to hold all of them at once.

**When to use:** When you have your own material to bring. Requires *Explicit* or *Implied* presence.

## TIER 3 — VOICE OPERATIONS
*Operations that change HOW the source speaks.*

### 12. Strategic mistranscription

**Move:** Reproduce parts of the source with deliberate small changes — a word swapped, a tense shifted, a name changed, a typo introduced or preserved.

**What it does:** Makes fidelity itself the subject. Forces the reader to ask: which is the document and which is the writer? Honors the source by making your relationship to it visible as a relationship.

**Tradition:** Sherrie Levine in visual art (rephotographing Walker Evans). Kathy Acker (rewriting Cervantes, Dickens). The whole Oulipo project. Anne Carson sometimes "translates" with deliberate slips.

**Applied to your source:** Reproduce a vital sign block — but one number is the temperature in your mother's hospital room. Reproduce the discovery scene — but one item in the inventory is yours. Reproduce *waste-down* once correctly, then once as *waist-down*, then once as *waste*. Let the reader notice.

**When to use:** Powerful late-piece move. Avoid if you're not ready to defend the slips as deliberate; otherwise reads as error.

---

### 13. Speaking-as

**Move:** Write a passage in the voice of someone in the document — but not yourself, and not the original writer. The father. The mother who isn't there. The neighbor who found him in 2023. The doctor.

**What it does:** Imagines what the document refused to include. Fills the gap that was the source's most charged refusal.

**Tradition:** Carol Ann Duffy, *The World's Wife* (every poem from a wife's perspective who wasn't given a voice in the original story). Margaret Atwood, *The Penelopiad*. Bhanu Kapil's persona work. Madeline Miller, *Circe*.

**Applied to your source:** Write a paragraph from the perspective of the father, in the moment of the fall. Write the conversation Mimi (the ex-wife) is not having about him. Write the neighbor's account of finding him in December 2023. Write the page the caretaker would have written if she weren't paid by the hour.

**Risk:** Can tip into ventriloquism, which can read as appropriation. Use sparingly, with awareness.

**When to use:** When the document's silences are louder than its statements and you have the ear to fill them.

---

### 14. Silence-marking

**Move:** Don't write the gap. Mark it. Use [ ] or — or [redacted] or a literal blank space or an ellipsis or *here is what the document does not say:*.

**What it does:** Makes refusal visible without filling it. The most disciplined and most honest move available.

**Tradition:** Layli Long Soldier, *Whereas*. M. NourbeSe Philip, *Zong!*. Caroline Bergvall, *Drift*. Most contemporary post-archival poetry.

**Applied to your source:** *The document does not describe the heartfelt moment of clarity.* Then a blank line. Then the next section. Or: a chapter titled "What He Said in His Last Lucid Moment" that contains only [        ].

**When to use:** Almost always available. Often the strongest possible response to material that exceeds what you can say.

## TIER 4 — META OPERATIONS
*Operations on the relationship between you and the source itself.*

### 15. Frame disclosure

**Move:** State, at the beginning or end of the piece, what the source is, where it came from, and what you're doing with it. This is not the conceit; this is acknowledging the conceit.

**What it does:** Refuses the reader's confusion about the document's status. Trades mystery for honesty.

**Tradition:** Bhanu Kapil's prefaces. Maggie Nelson's *Bluets* footnote-as-acknowledgment. Most academic creative nonfiction.

**Applied to your source:** *This is a document I found in my Google Drive. I do not know whose family this is. The man in this document is not my father. My father is alive in California and we do not speak. The man in this document is not my grandfather. My grandfather is dead.*

**Risk:** Can rob the work of its mystery. Can also be the move that makes the work possible.

**When to use:** Decide deliberately. Default for v1: *don't*. The conceit works harder when undisclosed. But know it's available.

---

### 16. Refusal of operation

**Move:** Name an operation you considered and refused to perform. Explain why.

**What it does:** Makes ethical limit visible as form.

**Tradition:** Bhanu Kapil throughout. Anne Boyer, *The Undying*. Jenny Offill in moments. The auto-theory tradition broadly.

**Applied to your source:** *I considered using his last words. I will not.* *I looked up the family. I did not save what I found.* *There is a sentence I wanted to write here about my mother. I haven't written it.*

**When to use:** When refusal is the most honest content you have. Powerful as a single late-piece move; dangerous if it becomes the whole piece (which becomes its own form of avoidance).

---

### 17. Source-as-character

**Move:** The document itself becomes a presence in the piece. Not its content — the *fact of the document*. Its arrival. Its file size. Its filename. Its metadata. The Drive folder it sits in.

**What it does:** Turns the meta-situation into part of the work. The reader is reading a piece about a piece about a man dying.

**Tradition:** *House of Leaves*. Borges. The whole found-object tradition.

**Applied to your source:** *Dad_20-_20Oct-Nov_202024_pdf.pdf. 14 pages. Modified date unknown to me. No ownership history visible. It sits in the same Drive folder as my tax returns from 2022.*

**When to use:** When you want the reader to feel the chance and uncanniness of the document's arrival. Often the opening or closing move of a piece.

## CHOOSING OPERATIONS

For Phase 4, you'll pick three. Some pairings work well together. Some compete.

**Pairings that work:**

- Erasure + Annotation (you erase a page, then annotate the erasure)
- Fragmentation + Voice-braiding (small units in two voices)
- Re-sequencing + Indexing (you reorder, then add the index that justifies the reorder)
- Annotation + Speaking-as (the document, plus a marginal voice that may be the father)
- Frame disclosure + Silence-marking + Refusal-of-operation (the most exposed and honest mode)
- Time-collapse + Voice-braiding + Image-juxtaposition (Sebald-style)

**Pairings that compete:**

- Erasure + Voice-braiding (erasure wants the source contained; voice-braiding wants the source one of two voices)
- Frame disclosure + Source-as-character (both do meta work; pick one)
- Strategic mistranscription + Fidelity to the source as object (pick a stance)

**Three is a ceiling for v1.** If you want to do everything, you're not doing v1. The Carson lineage is intoxicating. Cut.

## A NOTE ON ETHICS

This document was not written for you. The family has not given permission. They cannot give permission, because finding them to ask would itself be a violation of the conditions under which the document arrived. (See risk register, item 9, in `01_PROJECT-PLAN.md`.)

Found-text work has historically not asked permission. The legal frame is fair use for transformative creative work that doesn't identify the source. The ethical frame is more complicated and entirely yours.

A few principles that hold across the literary tradition:

1. **Transform.** The bar is whether the work does something the source could not do alone. Quotation is not transformation. Operations are.
2. **Don't identify.** Do not name the family. Do not include the date range that would make them findable. *Dad_20-_20Oct-Nov_202024_pdf.pdf* should not appear in the work as a literal filename. The podcast *Serialously* should not appear at all.
3. **Don't make money on the unaltered source.** This is a smaller risk for a self-published zine than for a book deal, but it's worth knowing. If v2 is a print run for sale, the relationship between the source and your transformations needs to be more decisive.
4. **Be willing to be challenged.** If, hypothetically, a member of this family ever found your zine — could you defend what you did? Not legally. Ethically. The answer doesn't have to be *they would approve*. The answer has to be *I can name what I did and why*.

The operations are how you become defensible. They are how the work stops being someone else's grief and becomes yours.

---

## END NOTE

The operations are tools. They are not the work.

The work is what you choose to do with them and why. The work is how you sit with the source long enough to know what it does and doesn't deserve. The work is your sentences, your selections, your silences, your refusals. The work is yours.

The toolkit just gives you the names.
