Part 01

What we're actually pitching

The concept locked at the scope we're pitching. One piece. Expansion held in the body.

The ask, precisely

One piece. One book. Pitched as the first in an ongoing format designed to be monthly, but asking now only for the first. BOTM's program is explicit: "pitch us an idea" → "we'll be in touch about the potential of putting our resources behind your idea" → "we will select creators we want to work with on an ongoing basis." The initial ask is always for one. Ongoing is earned, not pitched.

The one-line

A collage-essay — film plus companion printed essay — on the interior life of reading one contemporary literary novel, tied to a Book of the Month literary fiction or Volume 0 selection.

The concept paragraph

The piece is a monthly-viable collage-essay held by a reader-figure. It runs 6–9 minutes on film and ships alongside a short printed companion essay-object in the Book of the Month box. The form is collaged: moving image, still photograph, voiceover, writing on screen, film stills, handheld and webcam footage, music, the book as physical object — all composed simultaneously rather than sequentially. No segment structure, no declared grammar, no explanatory framing. The reader-figure appears in two registers — cinematic when the piece wants to hold her, webcam or handheld when the piece wants to hear her think. Her voice runs under everything. Her writing surfaces as text on screen. The book is the occasion; the reading is the subject.

Scope of the first piece

  • Film: 6–9 min collage-essay, digital distribution (social, BOTM site, creator feeds)
  • Companion printed essay: short, could ship with the BOTM box or live as standalone
  • Short cuts: 3 vertical cuts (atmospheric, voiced, text-forward) for social
  • Production budget: within the Creator-in-Residence $50–$5,000 range

Expansion language, held in the body of the pitch — not in the ask

The form is designed to be ongoing. Every piece is one artifact with durable monthly repeatability. If this first piece works, there is a natural path to a monthly column — adjacent to Volume 0 in publishing register, tied to BOTM's literary fiction selections in rhythm — which can scale or stay small depending on what lands with readers. I'm starting with one.

Sample book for this pitch

Lost Lambs by Madeline Cash (Book of the Month, January 2026). Used throughout the plan for sample-episode work, deck proof slides, and concrete language in Field 3. Replaceable later if a different book resonates more, but anchored here for the plan's sake.

Field 3 source paragraph · preserve through drafting
Sitting out on a balcony in Paris, watching the sitting reading and thinking and drinking coffee and smoking and writing in your journal while listening to music and perhaps talking passionately with a friend about the book you're reading and how it makes you feel — what you see vs. what they see, the poetry and prose it inspires in you, the change it inspires, how it touches your own life, how you see the world when you come out of the world within the book, how it immediately becomes nostalgic and fuzzy because you can never go back to the feeling of reading the book for the first time, how it feels to hold a book close to your heart, your mind, for it to inspire deep thought, feeling, and discussion, how it connects to the world.
Register note — hold this
This is not a European pitch. The balcony paragraph describes a quality of attention that Mia has experienced in Europe but that the piece translates into the reading life of any woman, anywhere. San Diego. Brooklyn. Mexico City. A bedroom at night. A park bench. Not aspirational — the actual reading life of BOTM's actual subscribers, filmed and written with the craft their reading deserves.
Part 02

The piece itself

What it contains, how it behaves, what the character is. Reference-free. This section is the piece described.

The four positions, held by collage, never declared

The piece holds four dimensions of reading simultaneously. It does not name them. The form does the work. The viewer experiences all four without knowing the taxonomy exists.

  • Interior — the reader's private consciousness during and after reading. Voiceover as thinking, held shots of the reader in her own space.
  • Response — the writing the reader does back. Prose, poetry, fragments, marginalia. Appears as text on screen, treated as a character.
  • Outward reading — the world the book has opened. Image-field, other texts, songs, photographs, film stills, associations. The book makes the world look different.
  • Transformation — the reader being changed. A motif that recurs across the piece — an image, a line, a color, a music cue — registering that the reader is not who she was when the piece started.

Vocabulary — what appears on screen

Not all in every piece. This is the range. A single piece draws six or eight.

Cinematic footage of the reader-figure

  • Composed shots of her reading in a specific light and space
  • Her hands — turning pages, writing in the margin, holding a cup, pressing a spine flat
  • Her face — in profile, in shadow, through a window, rarely to camera
  • Her moving through a space — walking, opening a door, sitting down with a book
  • Objects associated with her — a specific ring, a sweater, a room — that become iconographic over time

Webcam, handheld, casual

  • Webcam squares — her talking about a book with the unguardedness of a voice memo
  • Phone clips — the sky she saw, the street she walked, a cat on a bed
  • Selfie-ish footage — mirror, laptop, bathroom with her own hand holding the phone
  • Voice-memo audio over a static image — the sound of her actually recording her thoughts

Still photography and found images

  • Her own photographs, if she takes them
  • Found photographs — archive, old family images, scans from books
  • Film stills from films that resonate (fair use, properly sourced)
  • Paintings, photographs, art images surfaced by the book
  • Gifs — small looping moments, the old-internet vocabulary

Book-as-object

  • The book itself — cover, spine, specific pages, typography of a passage
  • Her underlines and marginalia
  • The book in context — on a table, in a bag, next to another book, on a pillow
  • Multiple books — the month's pick alongside what it opened

Text on screen

  • Her own writing — prose, poetry, fragments — appearing and staying
  • Handwritten notes, sometimes in her actual hand
  • Typed fragments in a specific font family, not subtitles
  • Quotes from the book, treated as visual objects
  • Annotations, marginalia, scanned pages of her notebook

Audio

  • Her voice in voiceover, always, as the connective tissue
  • Music — folk, ambient, minimalist, whatever the book asks for
  • Ambient sound — rain, a kettle, distant city, the analog hum of a room
  • Silence — held, on purpose, occasionally

Web elements, used sparingly — not as container

  • Browser windows, tabs, moodboard grids — when the drift goes outward into research
  • Pinterest-grid moments, Are.na-feel pages, search results as visual material
  • Old-web textures — ASCII, low-res jpgs, lo-fi gif — when the aesthetic calls

The reader-figure

A characterized version of Mia. Three modalities of presence:

  • Cinematic body — composed, deliberate, lit-with-intention footage. She is the subject. Camera holds.
  • Casual body — webcam, handheld, phone-clip. She is a real person in a real life. Unguarded.
  • Written word — her own writing appearing as text. A third form of her presence, non-bodily.

Voice runs under everything. The character is the constant across episodes; the book is the variable. She does not perform warmth — warmth enters when the material warrants it. She is thinking, and the viewer watches the thinking.

The companion printed essay-object

Short printed piece, maybe 4–16 pages or a folded broadsheet. Written essay or fragment-collection or hybrid — whatever the film asks for in print. Published under BOTM's imprint, Volume 0-adjacent in register. Shipped in the BOTM box for subscribers who select the literary fiction pick that month, or available as standalone object. This is the second artifact of the commission, and it is the move that converts a creator gig into a publishing credit.

Short cuts — three per film

  1. Atmospheric — no voice, single image or short sequence, single line of text, music. 60s max.
  2. Voiced — a single drift-movement from the film, 60–90s, voice over collaged image.
  3. Text-forward — a fragment of writing held on a single image. Almost a quote card, lifted by composition.

Not edited down from the film. Extracted — the collage disassembles natively.

Reader loop (optional, future expansion)

Each piece ends with an invitation: send me your drift from this book. Image, line, song, memory. Responses curated into a page on the BOTM site, a newsletter section, or a future zine. Activates the audience as co-authors. Addresses community-sentiment issues BOTM has had by making members feel addressed. Not in the initial pitch. Named as possible.

Part 03

Strategic context

BOTM as institution. Your lane inside their audience. Why the pitch fits now. Tensions named honestly.

BOTM is building an institution, not running a subscription box

Rebooted in 2016 for millennial and Gen Z women. 400,000+ members, 2M Instagram followers. In the last three years they have made four institutional moves that together change what BOTM is: the Lolly Award ($25K prize for best fiction, voted by engaged tier members, 200,000 votes per year); Volume 0 (short-fiction literary magazine, six issues a year, $35K author fees per issue, authors include Emma Cline, Nada Alic, Kristen Roupenian, Juliet Escoria, Brad Watson); the Aspen Literary Festival co-chair role (BOTM CEO John Lippman co-chairs with Editorial Director Brianna Goodman; partnership with Aspen Words, a 50-year-old literary nonprofit); and the Creator-in-Residence program itself.

Lippman's stated ambition for Aspen: "the literary love child of Sundance and South by Southwest." This is a brand positioning itself as a cultural institution with festival-scale and prize-giving-scale ambition. The Creator-in-Residence program is the content arm of that ambition. Your pitch lives in that ecosystem.

Your lane inside their audience

BOTM's audience is broad — it includes BookTok romantasy readers, domestic-thriller readers, celebrity memoir readers, YA fantasy readers. You are not pitching to the whole base. You are pitching to the serious-literary-fiction subset — the readers who pick Madeline Cash, Kiran Millwood Hargrave, Aja Gabel, Erin Somers, Emily Adrian, Beatriz Serrano, and the Volume 0 roster. This subset is substantial but specific. Naming your audience inside BOTM's audience is pitch maturity. Other applicants will claim universality. You will claim precision.

The pitch can say, explicitly or implicitly: "This is for the reader who thinks about a book for weeks after. Not the scroller, not the hater, not the rater. The underline-and-return reader."

Why this pitch fits now

BOTM has recently had reputational turbulence in the serious-reader segment of their base — the James Frey AI-novel selection drew criticism, there is ongoing subscriber frustration about credit policies. Volume 0, the Lolly, the Aspen festival, and this program are, visibly, moves to rebuild trust with serious readers. Your pitch quietly embodies the solution rather than naming the problem. You do not complain about BookTok. You do not critique their other content. You propose the thing they need to have in their content ecosystem — slow, rigorous, craft-forward, reader-centered — and let it speak for itself.

Tensions named honestly

Tension 1 — Not every BOTM pick suits this form

The form needs a book whose interior life is what the reader's relationship to it is about. That is true of literary fiction and much contemporary fiction. It is less true of Viking romantasy or domestic thriller. The pitch addresses this by scoping to BOTM's literary fiction / contemporary fiction / Volume 0 lane, not the whole catalog. This narrowing strengthens the pitch.

Tension 2 — Monthly is a real production rhythm

A 6–9 minute collage-essay with companion printed essay is real work. One per month is sustainable with resources. Twelve at once would not be. The pitch starts with one piece. Expansion is held as possibility, contingent on both sides wanting it.

Tension 3 — High-craft is a different production economics than BOTM's existing creator content

Most BOTM creator content is short-form and high-volume. Your piece is long-form and slow. The pitch positions this as flagship content, not filler. One piece a month that anchors their literary-fiction lane, promoted across formats, while their existing volume content continues. Your piece is the prestige object.

Tension 4 — The centennial is context, not frame

BOTM is 100 in 2026. Mentioning it once in the pitch body is fine — it signals you understand where the brand is in its institutional moment. Leading with the centennial would weaken the concept by making it campaign-tied. The piece needs to outlive the anniversary year. Keep the centennial light.

Strategic spine — one sentence
The piece does for video essay what BOTM does for literary fiction: it brings real rigor into a warm, accessible, monthly-rhythm form, for readers who want depth without gatekeeping.
Part 04

Reference library

Priority-tiered. Review the MUST-SEE items before Session 2 (sample episode sketch). Everything else feeds design and language across sessions 3–6.
Films and moving image — the essay-film / collage lineage
must-see Chantal Akerman · News from Home (1977) film
A woman alone, reading letters over static long takes of New York. The single closest structural ancestor to what you are making. The voice-over-image grammar, the trust in stillness, the refusal to rush. Free on Criterion Channel.
must-see Agnès Varda · The Gleaners and I (2000) film
First-person collage-documentary. Hands, objects, memory, filmed with warmth and rigor. The filmmaker as a readable character in her own film. Permission to include yourself without performing.
must-see Chris Marker · Sans Soleil (1983) film
The collage-essay-film at its formal peak. Voiceover over fragment, association without narrative spine. Watch for how the voice carries the piece without over-explaining. The Sandor Krasna frame is a direct model for your characterized reader-figure.
useful Moyra Davey · Les Goddesses (2011) film / art video
Davey reading Mary Wollstonecraft aloud while pacing her apartment. A woman's intellectual life filmed without apology. The closest art-world ancestor to the reader-figure you are building.
useful Arthur Jafa · Love is the Message, The Message is Death (2016) film / installation
Collage as emotional and political argument, found footage composed into a piece greater than its sources. Proof that essay-film can be both rigorous and popularly moving.
useful Jonas Mekas · As I Was Moving Ahead Occasionally I Saw Brief Glimpses of Beauty (2000) film
Diary-collage. The daily as material for art. Long and associative — skim, do not finish. For texture of the unguarded/handheld register.
useful Lynne Ramsay · Morvern Callar (2002) film
A young woman, her interior, her drift, filmed with analog warmth and grain. Proof the aesthetic can be both art-cinema and emotionally grounded. The body-in-space register you want.
deep-dive Harun Farocki · Images of the World (1988) film
Rigorous essay-film, colder register. Background context for how the form thinks-by-juxtaposing. Do not try to match this tone — study the method.
deep-dive Sofia Coppola · Somewhere (2010) film
Quiet, textural, a woman thinking. For the pacing register and unstyled-interior texture.
Literary — collage-essay, hybrid-genre, first-person-as-sensibility
must-see Anne Carson · Nox (2010) book-object
A book in a box. Translation, elegy, and book-making as parallel acts. The object-as-reading-event. Direct ancestor of the companion printed essay-object concept.
must-see Maggie Nelson · Bluets (2009) book
240 numbered fragments on the color blue, grief, reading, Wittgenstein. The four positions held simultaneously in prose. If you can do this in your printed essay, you have the register.
useful W.G. Sebald · The Rings of Saturn (1995) book
Prose with embedded photographs. Walking, reading, association. A whole book is a collage. For the pacing of thought.
useful Rachel Cusk · Outline trilogy (2014–2018) novels
First-person narration that recedes so other people come forward. The listening reader. Relevant for the reader-figure's mode of attention — present but not centered.
useful Joe Brainard · I Remember (1970) book
Collage-book of memory fragments. The form of remembering, made literary. Very close in spirit to what a printed essay-object could be.
useful Patti Smith · M Train (2015) memoir
Reader in her apartment, reader in cafes, reader in memory. Characterized-first-person done with total confidence. The exact register of a woman who is legibly a reader.
useful Kate Briggs · This Little Art (2017) book
An essay written while translating Barthes. The reading and the writing happening in parallel. Proof that essay-about-reading can be itself a form of reading.
useful Claudia Rankine · Citizen (2014) book
Text, photograph, white space as composition. A book that has a film's rhythm in print.
deep-dive Teju Cole · Blind Spot (2017) book
Photograph and prose together, neither subordinate. Model for how image and text can co-carry meaning in the companion essay-object.
Visual art — collage as practice
useful John Stezaker photomontage
Photographic collage, minimal, precise, haunted. Single images that do enormous psychological work. For the short-cut text-forward card.
useful Wangechi Mutu collage
Collage as figure, body, transformation. For the visual ambition — high art can still be warm and figural.
useful Rinko Kawauchi photography
Quiet, light-drenched, ordinary moments treated as sacred. The attention-to-domestic-ordinariness register the piece wants without cozy-ness.
deep-dive Hannah Höch, Kurt Schwitters, Romare Bearden classical collage
Foundational collage — the modernist and Harlem Renaissance canons. Look for reference when the compositional logic of a scene isn't working.
deep-dive Sally Mann, Nan Goldin photography
For the analog-grain, intimate-interior register of the cinematic body. Permission to film domestic space with real attention.

Session plan

Seven sessions, ~9 hours total. Each session has a minimum viable version if energy is low. Click checkboxes to track progress. Click phase headers to collapse.
Entry-point flexibility
The sessions are numbered but not strictly sequential. Recommended order: Phase 1 → 2 → 3 → 4 → 5 → 6 → 7. If energy pulls you toward writing before reference-gathering, flip Phase 1 and Phase 3. PHASE 2Sample episode sketch
~90 min · writing mode · minimum viable: list of 6–8 fragments
The hardest creative task. Doing it now — while energy is fresh — protects every downstream session. The sample does two jobs: it proves the concept to you before it proves it to BOTM, and it becomes Slide 3 material + Field 3 language.
Setup
  • 2.1
    New Google Doc: "Marginalia — Sample Episode · Lost Lambs" (or whatever working name).
  • 2.2
    Paste your Phase 1 caught line at the top. This is the seed.
  • 2.3
    Below it, write one sentence on why the line caught. Do not explain to a reader. Explain to yourself.
The sketch — treat as a director's treatment, not a script
  • 2.4
    Write the opening image. What does the viewer see first? Describe it in 2–3 sentences. Register, not detail.
  • 2.5
    Write the book, introduced. How does the book arrive in the piece? Voiceover? A held shot? A cut to a page?
  • 2.6
    Write four or five drift movements. Each movement is a paragraph. What image, writing, association, song, memory, or text surfaces?
    • Don't force four positions — let them be implicit
    • Don't narrate the book's plot
    • Let the drift genuinely drift
  • 2.7
    Write the return. How does the piece come back to the book and the reader? What is the closing image or line?
  • 2.8
    Write a short paragraph on the companion essay-object — what is in print? A poem you wrote? A fragment of prose? A single image? One design choice (folded broadsheet? saddle-stitched? single card?).
  • 2.9
    List the three short cuts. One atmospheric, one voiced, one text-forward. Just two lines each.
Close
  • 2.10
    Read the whole sketch cold. Does it feel like the thing you've been describing? If not, note one thing off. Do not fix now.
  • 2.11
    Save. Close. Phase 2 complete.

  4. A visual or technical project from your portfolio — the strongest generative-AI or creative-technology piece you have ready.

Field 2 — Type of content you're best at
Dropdown: Stills, Memes, Short-form series, Street content/interviews, Other

Pick Other. Write in: Hybrid long-form essay-film and printed literary essay-object. Direction, writing, and collage-based production.

Do not pick Short-form series — you are not pitching short-form. Pick Other and define yourself.

Field 3 — The pitch
"Pitch us an idea you want to make with us. Be as specific as possible. Think outside the box. Your ideas do not have to center around books, but rather content that would appeal to our audience of Gen Z/Millennial women who love to read."

Target: 400–600 words. Four-move structure:

  1. Open with the sensibility in motion. 2–3 sentences that put the reader inside the register. Pull from the balcony paragraph.
  2. Define the form. One paragraph on what the piece is: a monthly-viable collage-essay, film + companion printed essay, tied to BOTM's literary fiction selections, Volume 0-adjacent in register.
  3. Name the lane. One paragraph on who the piece is for — the literary-fiction subset of BOTM's audience. Name the taste cluster (Madeline Cash, Kiran Millwood Hargrave, Aja Gabel, Emma Cline, etc.).
  4. Close with the ask. You are pitching one piece first. Name expansion as possibility. End on a single line restating what the piece is.

Avoid:

  • Opening with "I propose" or "I want to make"
  • Pitch-deck language ("I'll deliver," "my deliverables are")
  • Quantitative promises ("will get X views," "engagement will be")
  • Over-leaning on the centennial
  • Cozy/me-time/book-club framing

Keep:

  • Your actual sentence rhythm
  • Literary references where they fit naturally
  • Specificity about what makes the form different
  • Direct acknowledgment that BOTM's program brief explicitly invited this kind of pitch
Field 4 — Production budget

Part 07

Deck structure

Slide-by-slide direction. What each slide does, what it shouldn't do, image territory, copy guidance.

Deck principle

The deck must be the thing it pitches. If it looks like a standard pitch deck — clean corporate sans, bullet lists, one-color accents — the pitch is dead before it is read. It should look like a literary object in the Volume 0 register. Warm paper tones, serif typography, breathing white space, one atmospheric image per slide doing real work, single-accent color. Think Kinfolk-meets-Cabinet-meets-Aperture, not McKinsey.
SLIDE 1
Atmosphere. Not a title slide. A mood slide.
JOB
Make the reader feel the piece before reading anything explanatory.
CONTENT
One image (film-still register, or high-quality photograph). One phrase 5–10 words max. That's it.
AVOID
Title text. Deck title. Logo. "Pitch deck" anywhere on the slide.
SLIDE 2
Concept. One-line and the definitional paragraph.
JOB
Name what the piece is. Name the form. Name the lane.
CONTENT
Working title at top. One-line in middle, large. 60-word concept paragraph below. One small image or one elegant rule/divider. Volume 0 mentioned in passing.
AVOID
Bullet lists. More than 100 words total. Explanatory diagrams.
SLIDE 3
A sample month — Lost Lambs. Proof of execution.
JOB
Show you can actually do the thing.
CONTENT
Book title as header. 4–5 fragment-captions for the drift movements (from Phase 2). 4–5 small image slots beside them. One line naming the companion essay-object. One line naming the three short cuts.
AVOID
Plot summary of the book. Timeline diagrams. Over-explaining the drift.
SLIDE 4
The system. Cross-format commission distilled.
JOB
Show this is not content — it is an ongoing format that uses BOTM's existing infrastructure.
CONTENT
Five surfaces: film → essay-object → short cuts → reader loop → (possible) zine. One line each. Small visual diagram showing how they relate. Volume 0 named as the adjacent publishing sibling.
AVOID
Org charts. Rollout timelines. Financial projections. "Phase 1, Phase 2" corporate-deck language.
SLIDE 5
Bio + close. Short positioning. Final line.
JOB
Position Mia credibly in 3–4 sentences. Close with a line that stays.
CONTENT
Bio paragraph: writer, senior copywriter at Google Brand Studio (Google Maps × Benson Boone / Marías credits), creative technologist in formation. Closing line (TBD — emerges from Field 3 drafting).
AVOID
Resume-style bullet list. Client logo wall. Headshot. "Thanks for reading." Timeline of how the project rolls out.

Typography direction

Serif body. No sans-serif body. Candidates: GT Sectra, Tiempos, Canela, IBM Plex Serif. Optional mono accent for captions and callouts — IBM Plex Mono matches this plan's aesthetic and ties visually to your existing project work.

Hierarchy: one large display face for titles and key pull-quotes, one body face, one mono for caption/metadata. No more than three typefaces total.

Palette direction

Warm base — cream, parchment, off-white. Not pure white. Text in warm-black or deep ink-brown, not pure #000. One accent color, used sparingly — a dusty teal, a deep olive, a muted rust. Pulled from your existing moodboard register (forest-tones + retrofuturistic cool per your aesthetic guide).

Image direction

Every image earns its place. No stock illustration. No AI-generated generic imagery. No placeholder shapes. Each image is doing real emotional or intellectual work. Sources: film stills (fair use for pitch), personal photography, high-quality photojournalism or art photography licensed properly.

If an image is not available that does the job — leave the slot empty with a single line of text instead. A white page with one sentence is stronger than a slide with a weak image.
