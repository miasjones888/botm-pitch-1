# DEEP ANNOTATION — A Close Reading of the Found Document
*A literary annotation pass. Not a project plan. This is the document, read slowly, with attention to form, image, rhythm, gap, and resonance. Read once. Annotate the annotations.*

---

## Preliminary observations on the document as object

Before any single page: **what is this thing**.

It is a Google Doc, almost certainly. Exported to PDF. The formatting tells: variable line breaks, the slightly-off paragraph spacing, the inconsistent capitalization of timestamps (*"6:50 am"* vs *"7:17am"* vs *"6:50 am:"*). No template. No header style. The default font (probably Arial or Times). No page numbers. The PDF is 15 pages but the document does not behave like a 15-page document — it behaves like a single scrolling note that someone exported because someone needed to send it somewhere.

Consider what this means: **this was written to be read.** Not by clinicians, not by lawyers, not for the chart. By family. The "Dear Family" letter at the end confirms the audience but the form was already telling you. Multiple authors (Jay clearly, Amy and Annie occasionally, the caretaker for several days), shared editing access, real-time updates. This is a **collaborative grief document**. The form is itself unprecedented or near-unprecedented in literature. There is no genre called "the family group log written during a parent's hospitalization." But everyone who has been through this has produced one. Most are lost.

This one is in your Google Drive.

**The first question of the piece — the question the piece *exists to ask* if you let it — is: how did this arrive?** The piece is allowed to never answer. The not-answering may be the entire move.

## Page 1 — Recent history for context

> *"Dad has declined in ability to physically move for the last several years."*

The first word is *Dad*. Not *Our father*, not *Dr. ——*, not *Patient John D*. Immediately the document declares itself: this is the family register. The clinical and the intimate are about to braid for fifteen pages and the first word is the side they pick.

> *"He refuses help and lies about events like these, trying to hide any concerns."*

Already, in sentence two, the central characterological move: *refusal*. The book is going to be about a man whose dying is shaped by the same refusal that shaped his living. *Refuses help. Lies about events. Hides concerns.* The verbs are doing all the work. He is the active agent of his own concealment up until the moment he can't be anymore.

This will resonate with your grandfather material — the cruel man who let himself get too sick to recover. The pattern of the refused rescue. **Mark this for your reaction capture.**

> *"There were several falls and strokes we found out about that were hidden from us since 2014 (3 known) and unsure of how many have occurred without our knowledge."*

The parenthetical is doing a lot. *3 known.* The acknowledgment that there is a knowable number and an unknowable number. The shape of estranged caretaking: you are reconstructing your parent's decline backwards from evidence, like an archaeologist.

> *"He was outside on the ground for over 2 hours until a neighbor found him."*

The neighbor as the only witness. Recurring motif: people who are not family doing the work of family. The mobile groomer. The professional cleaner. The Brightstar Care nurse. The neighbor. Eventually: the police, the EMTs, the nurses, the hospitalist, the speech therapist, the cardiologist, Tianna. **The family arrives last to the body of their father, and only because strangers got there first.**

This is the central structural irony of contemporary American dying. Worth sitting with.

> *"This has spiraled into terrible hygiene, bills going unpaid (unable to get the mail and refusing to sign up for autopay), thus service(s) turning off, medication not getting delivered (thus not taken), etc."*

The chain of causation as one sentence. *Hygiene → mail → bills → service → medication → not-taken.* The voice is operational. *Etc.* says: there is more, and the writer is too tired to list it.

The autopay refusal is so specific. A man who would rather his power get cut off than authorize automatic withdrawal. The bipolar father. The man who cannot release control of any small thing.

> *"This visit was primarily focused on selling him on the dream of moving back home and leaving Vegas."*

*Selling him on the dream.* The marketing language is almost shocking next to *we got his best friend Ken involved to push and he still declined.* The family deploying every persuasive tool available — emotional, logistical, social — to sell their father on his own rescue. He doesn't buy.

> *"This is especially frustrating because his manic depressive disorder (Bipolar) disallows him from feeling feelings."*

A theory of his interiority, declared flat. *Disallows him from feeling feelings.* The diagnostic framing being used to make sense of why love does not move him. Whether this is true clinically is beside the point — what matters is that this is how the family has decided to understand him. The bipolar diagnosis is the family's working theory of why their father cannot be reached.

**Your father has bipolar, unmedicated. The same theory may have been the working theory in your house. This sentence is going to ring loud. Sit with how loud.**

> *"Anytime we sense an ounce of vulnerability or acceptance, he instantly becomes a hard exterior."*

The hard exterior. Possibly the most-used phrase in adult children of personality-disordered or bipolar parents. The sentence has the worn quality of something said many times before.

## Page 1, second half — Dad's communication with kids

The shift from narrative to surveillance log is jarring and important.

> *"We have 'read receipts' turned on his phone so we know if he was able to physically check a message."*
> *"We also have 'Find My' activated to see if the phone ever leaves the house."*
> *"We have a Life Alert unit set up in the house and emergency call buttons in high risk areas (ie. shower)."*

This is a description of a remote-monitoring system jerry-rigged out of consumer technology. The family has **converted his phone into a presence detector** because he won't talk to them. The terms of the relationship: he refuses connection, so they install instruments. The instruments are how they love him.

This is one of the most quietly devastating passages in the document. It also describes an entirely contemporary form of caretaking — one that did not exist twenty years ago and that defines aging-parent care in the smartphone era. There is a whole essay in this. *You don't have to write that essay.* But you should notice that the document already wrote it without realizing.

> *"His daily routine is in/out of sleep all day/night (often seeing read receipts at 2am), sitting in front of the TV, drinking, and stepping out to the garage to smoke a cigarette roughly every hour or so."*

The routine described entirely from inference. They never see him. They construct him from pings.

The cigarette. The drinking. The grandfather who smoked. The pattern.

## Page 2 — Wednesday 10/23/24

> *"6am: Annie texts Jay asking if he's heard from Dad."*

The opening of the medical-log proper. **The form changes.** From paragraph to timestamped log. The narration tightens. We are now in the document's second voice — Jay-as-medical-historian.

The pacing of the timestamps is important. *6am → 7:02am → 7:40am → 8:23am.* Two hours and twenty-three minutes from "have you heard from Dad" to "Police and ambulance arrive to find Dad on the ground."

> *"He is rushed to EMERGENCY at Mountain View Hospital."*

The capitalized EMERGENCY. The shouted word.

> *"While he had an IV, his mouth and throat were completely hardened with thick mucus crusted onto all surfaces, unable to be pried off. The nurse and doctor had described it as waxy to Amber and no one could get it off. It sounded like a sock was in his throat."*

This is the first piece of literary writing in the document. The simile *sounded like a sock was in his throat* is precise and terrible. *Waxy* is the doctor's word. *Hardened with thick mucus crusted onto all surfaces* is medical-precise but the rhythm — *hardened, thick mucus, crusted, all surfaces* — is doing something. The sentence is hard to read aloud. That is the point. The throat itself is hard to read.

> *"Annie and Jay put on gloves, got them wet, and began trying to clear his mouth."*

The children performing the work the hospital won't. Will. The first inversion of the parent-child care dynamic. They are wiping out his mouth. He used to wipe out theirs.

> *"After about 30 minutes, Dad came to and said 'Feels like Heaven'"*

**This is the first chorus.** Bookmark this line. It will return. It is in many ways the most important single line in the document.

The dash before the next clause — *we continued and stayed with him all day although he was not really aware of where he was or what was going on* — performs the deflation. The miracle moment is two words long and then the work continues.

> *"7:00pm: Amy, Annie, and Jay go to Dad's house to see what had happened. TV still on, blood on the floor, hardened feces, disintegrated diapers, dead skin on the bed, moved furniture, broken trash can he must have fell on, etc. Overall, a very horrific scene to walk into. Fireplace and gas on. We cleaned it up, grabbed his phone and a few belongings."*

The discovery scene. **Read this aloud.** It scans as a list-poem. *TV still on / blood on the floor / hardened feces / disintegrated diapers / dead skin on the bed / moved furniture / broken trash can he must have fell on / etc.* The *etc* is the writer's exhaustion. The *fireplace and gas on* is added as a separate sentence — it is its own horror, kept apart.

The closing — *We cleaned it up, grabbed his phone and a few belongings* — collapses the trauma into the to-do. They are operational. They have to be. The flatness is the only way through.

Form note: this list-with-etc structure recurs throughout the document and is the closest thing to a formal device. **If you write into this document, the inventory list is one of its native forms.**

> *"Note: When recovering his phone and booting it back up, it appears the last read message was on Wednesday 10/16."*

The forensic Note. Jay breaks out of the timestamp form to give context. **The Notes are where the narrator becomes visible.** Track them. Each Note is a moment when the reporter steps onto the stage.

> *"Unsure of how many total days he was on the ground unable to move without food or water."*

The central unanswerable. Possibly four days. Possibly seven. The kidney doctor will later say it can't have been more than a few. The evidence says it must have been more. The document never resolves this and its refusal to resolve it is one of its strongest moves.

**What does it mean to not know how long your father was on the floor?** This is a question the piece can ask and refuse to answer.

## Page 3 — Thursday 10/24/24

> *"4:40am - Jay gets a call from the hospital that Dad is awake. We all 3 tell him we're next door and will be right there. His speech very impaired, still sounds like a sock in the throat, he says 'OK Great!'"*

The exclamation point. The cheerful affirmation through the impaired throat. *OK Great!* is a small, unbearable thing. It sounds like the dad. It sounds like *who he was*, surviving the sock.

> *"6:00am - Amy and Annie visit with Dad but he's very confused and disoriented. Asks the same question repeatedly and does not remember anything. Likely has no idea who is talking to him."*

The recursion begins.

> *"8:00am - Jay relieves Amy and Annie so they can go to Denver for an overnight to do a live Serialously show."*

Wait. **Stop here.** This sentence, in the middle of the medical log, casually drops that Amy and Annie host a podcast called *Serialously* and have a live show in Denver. Two of the three siblings are entertainment professionals. The document is being written by people whose day jobs include narrative production. *That* is why it is so unconsciously well-shaped.

This also tells you: **the document was probably saved into your Drive because someone shared it.** Possibly via a mass family/friends email distribution. Possibly someone forwarded it to you. Possibly you were on a list. The *Serialously* detail is searchable; if you wanted to know who this family is, you could find them in under a minute. **Decide consciously whether you want to know.** Not knowing may be load-bearing for the piece. Knowing may close it.

> *"Jay walks into Dad's room finding him eating a receipt with one hand and has a fork backwards in the other."*

The sentence is perfectly built. *Eating a receipt with one hand. Has a fork backwards in the other.* The two simultaneous failures. The receipt being eaten — paper as food — is doing several things at once: the literal hunger, the loss of object recognition, the hospital's failure to attend.

> *"At this stage he can barely move and his vision is completely impaired. As i stepped in to start feeding him he continued to grab at the plate and tried eating it like a pizza."*

The lowercase *i*. Probably a typo. Possibly fatigue. Functions as a small loss of authorial self.

> *"He repeatedly put his hands and gown in his mouth thinking it was food, despite how many times I told him not to."*

The reversal of feeding. The father is now the child eating the wrong things. *Despite how many times I told him not to* is a sentence a parent says about a toddler.

> *"He's in a desperate state of starvation and thirst."*

> *"Every few minutes Dad would ask me, 'Where's Jay?'"*

**The chorus deepens.** *Where's Jay?* asked of Jay. The question that contains its own answer it cannot recognize. This recurs through the document. By the end, *Where's Jay* is one of the load-bearing phrases of the entire fifteen pages.

> *"11:00am - In-home care services consultation with Francesca - she spoke to Dad to quiz him."*

The quiz. The cognitive testing as conversational form.

> *"He continues to remember his name and birthdate but that's all. She asked 'Who's this guy?' pointing to me and Dad responded, 'This is Bud, he's a local in Vegas who I met on my way down here.'"*

**Maybe the most literarily charged single passage.** He has invented a backstory for his son. He has placed Jay in the local Vegas community. He has constructed *a story for Jay's existence* because the actual story is not available to him. The brain confabulating. The novelist's brain confabulating. *Bud, a local in Vegas who I met on my way down here.* It is its own short story. It implies a journey. *Down here.* Where is *here*? Where did the dad think he was traveling from?

> *"She said, 'I like to call him Jay' - his eyes glazed over and didn't respond. Very confused."*

The introduction of the real name does not retrieve the real son. The glaze. The closing-down.

> *"12:00pm-3:00pm - Trying to sleep but very agitated, tossing and turning, not comfortable, removing clothing, tugging at his catheter (eventually pulling it off). The nurses aren't strong enough to help so Jay is working with them to change his diapers, and move him when needed."*

The son changes his father's diapers. Plain sentence. Moves on.

> *"Dad's agitation is showing with hallucinations. He thought the year was 1988 and did not believe it was 2024. He keeps saying we're teasing him and we keep assuring him we're not."*

*1988.* Pick a number, any number — the brain has chosen this one. What was 1988 for him? (Don't speculate in the piece. The number is enough.)

> *"They say it's best to keep reminding him of where he is and who he is to get him properly oriented."*

This will be reversed three days later when the family stops the quizzing because it makes him cry. The medical advice will be silently abandoned in favor of mercy.

> *"He asked me where his dad was a few times, made references to his brother Jim trying to kill him (note: Jim was psychotic and did try to kill his father). Both have been deceased for a long time."*

The parenthetical detonation. *Jim was psychotic and did try to kill his father.* A whole genealogy of mental illness, violence, and unresolved trauma is buried in a parenthetical clarification. The document cannot stop to deal with this. The piece could.

The grandfather of the document was almost killed by his son. The son in the document is now dying. The grandson Jay is changing his father's diaper. **Three generations of male inheritance compressed into a single afternoon's hallucinations.** Mark this for resonance.

> *"Dad kept saying he was being haunted and didn't believe I was Jay even though I kept reassuring him it was me and he's okay. He continued to insist he's on his deathbed and did not believe there's a way out."*

He was right. There wasn't. The document doesn't say so.

> *"He made a handgun gesture with his fingers and put it in his mouth."*

Single sentence. Own paragraph in spirit if not on page. **The starkest line in the document.** No comment. No reaction from the family. The writer reports it and moves on.

The discipline of moving on. What it costs. What it makes possible.

> *"He's going through spurts of coming to and insisting he's on his deathbed, talking about past people and events in the present, and sleeping."*

Three states. Coming to / insisting / sleeping. The triplet rhythm.

## Page 4 — Friday 10/25/24

> *"His eyes stare blindly wide open saying 'Hello?' when trying to communicate with him. I imagine he can't see more than a couple feet in front of him."*

The blind hello. The hesitation that is also a question.

> *"8:30am - Jay feeds him purees and water and Dad is in Heaven continuing to gasp 'That's delicious' and 'Tastes like Heaven'"*

**The chorus, again.** *Heaven* is now in the prose itself — *Dad is in Heaven* — not just in the quoted speech. The narrator has absorbed his father's word. The child takes the parent's vocabulary back.

> *"Dad asks why he's so thirsty and asks what happened. Jay tells him he fell and was on the ground for several days without food or water. He bursted into tears."*

*Bursted.* The grammatical irregularity is the writer's grief surfacing in the syntax. This is a sentence where the writer is no longer fully in control of his own grammar. **Mark this. It is the kind of micro-failure that authenticates the document beyond any rhetorical move.**

> *"At this point, I don't think he even remembers that conversation."*

The grief of the moment is also forgotten. The man cries about news he cannot keep. **The piece could be entirely organized around the figure of the unkeepable.** Memory as the thing leaving. The losses he sustains and then loses access to.

> *"He's also asked 'Where's your mom' and other questions that the answers don't make sense to him about."*

*Where's your mom.* The mother not present. Her name will arrive next: Mimi. *Mehmee.* The mother who is missing from the document is being summoned by the dying husband who does not remember she is missing.

This is your hinge. **If you enter the piece with your mother material, this is the gap it enters through.**

> *"When Jay asks questions to try and gauge mental awareness, it is clear he's completely delusional. Jay asked, 'Dad, who is my wife? Who am I married to?' He said 'Mimi' (pronounced 'Mehmee'). Jay said, 'No, that was your YOUR wife.' He said, 'When?' and started crying not knowing why she's not anymore."*

The capitalized YOUR. The emphasis transcribed. The grammar collapse in *that was your YOUR wife*. *When?* is a one-word sentence delivering a whole loss. He doesn't know when he stopped being married. The temporal scaffolding of his life has come down.

> *"He asks, 'Is she mad at me?' - Jay says 'No, no one is mad at you.' He says, 'There are lots of reasons for them to be' and continues crying."*

**The single most morally clear sentence in the document.** *There are lots of reasons for them to be.* He knows. Underneath the bipolar refusal, underneath the hard exterior, underneath the lying-about-the-falls, he knows what he has cost the people who love him. He says it once, briefly, and forgets. The piece could be organized around this single sentence. It contains everything the document cannot otherwise say.

It also contains the deepest resonance with your own material. *Lots of reasons for them to be.* The whole question of the cruel parent's awareness. The question of whether your grandfather knew. Whether your father, in his unmedicated bipolar, has any version of this clarity. **Sit with this line. Don't write toward it yet. Just sit.**

> *"Each one of those episodes lasts no more than a minute."*

The clinical close on the moral disclosure. Grief on a timer.

> *"11:00am - Kidney doctor visits. Sodium levels remain high and are being monitored every 6 hours."*

The medicine returns. The tide goes out from the moral revelation. The body's chemistry resumes its claim on the page.

## Page 5–6 — Saturday 10/26

> *"Dad is not in his room."*

The plot point. He has been moved without notification.

> *"Originally, they said this is good to reorient him to reality, but now it feels more like dementia."*

The medical advice pivot. The family's recognition that no amount of reorientation is going to retrieve their father. They stop asking him what year it is.

> *"At this point, Dad's worst fear is being realized and often times when he's awake he's asking how much longer even though he really has no idea where he is or how long it's been; he just acknowledges that he's in bad shape and fearful of the situation."*

He doesn't know where he is. He knows he is afraid. The body knows what the mind has lost.

> *"Amy and Annie went to Target to print out photos of us to familiarize him more and to have in his room when we're away."*

The Target run. The 8x10s. **The most contemporary detail in the document.** The way modern American families solve grief problems with errands.

> *"His speech is MUCH BETTER. Practically crystal clear."*

The capitalized hope.

> *"He says his throat is 'just a little dry' but when asked if he wants more water he said 'not right now' - this is great as the last few days he would not be one to decline water. Lights back off and back to sleep. I imagine he's sleeping 20 hours a day right now."*

The micro-improvement. The hope turned upward. *I imagine he's sleeping 20 hours a day.* Even the recovery is mostly absence.

> *"Seems like his left eye works but not the right."*

> *"While he likely won't be able to see them because they're at a distance, hopefully while his vision improves he will."*

Hope syntax: *likely won't … hopefully will.* The construction is doing the family's emotional labor.

> *"After going through Dad's emails, texts, and mobile app history, we have confirmed the last ordered meal was via Uber Eats on Tuesday 10/15."*

The forensic timeline. The Uber Eats receipt as last meal. **A modern document of dying.** What he last ate, when his phone last pinged, when his mail last got read. This is what death now leaves behind. Logs.

The piece could lean hard into this: *the contemporary inventory of a death*. The accounts that go cold. The subscriptions that auto-renew. The smart devices that report on an empty house.

> *"Annie spoke with the officer who was at the scene to tend to Dad and he clarified they did not move any furniture and worked on him in the laundry room where he was found then took him outside. This indicates that the fall did not happen in the laundry room as the chair was moved, clothes were removed, and blood was on the floor in the living room. He may have had multiple falls and it's possible his vision was blurry at that time as toys for Emmy and Theo were opened, Amazon packages had paper scraps on the ground, etc. Unsure as to what he may or may not thought was food/water in this delirious state. Seems he was likely crawling to try and get outside which would put him in the laundry room where he was found."*

The reconstruction. The detective work. **He was likely crawling.** Crawling toward outside. Toward the mail. Toward whatever he could not reach. The 2019 South Coast Plaza army-crawl returns: he has been trying to reach things from the floor for years.

The opened toys for the grandchildren on the floor. The Amazon paper scraps. The set design of his solitude. He was alone among objects intended for children he could not see.

> *"Amazon packages had paper scraps on the ground, etc. Unsure as to what he may or may not thought was food/water in this delirious state."*

The grammar slipping again — *what he may or may not thought*. The not-fixing of small errors as evidence of the writer not having time to be a writer.

> *"He was completely naked waste-down in the laundry room, only having a shirt on."*

*Waste-down.* The misspelling for *waist-down*. **It is the most accurate misspelling in the document.** What had been below his waist had also been waste. The body's evacuation. The living room floor. The hardened feces noted earlier. The misspelling does the whole thing without doing the whole thing.

If you ever want to write directly about this document, *waste-down* is a title or section heading.

> *"We logged into the Ring Doorbell app on Dad's phone to see the footage, but the camera is offline (batteries must be dead)."*

Every monitoring device in his life has failed. The Ring batteries dead. The phone off for days. The Life Alert he would not press. The neighbor who happened by. The system installed to keep him knowable failed exactly when there was something to know.

This pattern is one of the strongest formal motifs in the document. **You could index every failure-of-monitoring across the fifteen pages.** It would be its own list-poem.

## Pages 7–9 — Sunday 10/27 to Monday 10/28 (caretaker voice)

The shift to Tianna's caretaker notes is **the document's most significant formal move**. The voice changes completely. The daughter/son voice is replaced by the professional caregiver voice. The change is not announced; it just happens. Same document, totally different prose.

> *"8:50 am: on arrival client was sleeping and family gave me update on client."*

*Client.* No longer *Dad.* The clinical noun.

The caretaker's notes are themselves a form. They run vertically as a time log. *9:06 am … 9:30 am … 9:52 am … 10:49 am.* The intervals tighten when something is happening. They stretch when he's sleeping. The form is the attention.

The vital sign blocks are particularly powerful because of their flat repetition:

> *"Vitals 134/71. 64 pulse 94% oxygen 36.4 degrees Celsius (97.5 degree fahrenheit)"*
> *"8:14 am: 165/65 pulse 64 oxygen 95% temp 36.7 degree Celsius ( 97.7 degrees Fahrenheit)"*
> *"7:49 am: vitals 114/62 pulse 84 oxygen 92% Temp 37.1 degrees Celsius (98.7 degrees Fahrenheit)"*

These are small incantations. They have the rhythm of the rosary. **Lifted intact, formatted as poetry, they are already poetry.** Anne Carson would lift them.

Within the caretaker's voice: tiny, devastating bursts.

> *"11:12 am: internal medicine doctor came in with MRI results. Client had stroke."*

**The MRI revelation in fifteen words.** No emphasis. No reaction. The family is given the diagnosis through the caretaker's note. The most consequential medical news of the document arrives in the same flat register as *changed brief* and *finished ensure shake*.

> *"Will need round the clock care moving forward."*

The future contracts to a single phrase.

> *"2:38 pm: neurologist came in and did a check. She made sure his meds were correct and checked his movements."*

The brief professional encounters. He is being checked at intervals.

> *"6:50 pm: client had a minor episode. Was very quick to turn it around. He did rip a pillow because he was 'opening a bag of chips' because he was 'getting ready'. He did attempt to get out of bed but didn't even make it to the edge of the bed before snapping out of it and curling back up with the blanket."*

**Read this slowly.** *Opening a bag of chips because he was getting ready.* The double quotation. The hallucination is not just an image — it has a *purpose*. He was getting ready. To go where? For what? The hallucination has its own internal logic; he was acting on a plan from another time. The pillow as bag of chips. The motion of opening. The body remembering what the mind has invented.

This sentence is one of the great lines in the document. **The doubled quotation marks — *"opening a bag of chips" because he was "getting ready"* — give it the rhythm of nested confusion.** The caretaker is reporting a man whose actions had reasons that did not exist.

> *"Did not like them said not enough flavor."*

About the mashed potatoes. The aesthetic complaint surviving the cognitive collapse. He still has standards.

> *"During test client was experiencing left side aphasia and was bringing up past memories."*

The swallow test. He's eating barium-laced applesauce while a radiologist tracks the bolus down his esophagus. While this is happening he is talking about his past. The medical procedure as memory machine. The barium tracking food while the brain tracks decades. Both are imaging.

This image is one I would lift and use as-is. **The swallow test as memory test.**

## Page 11 — the cardiologist passage and Wednesday 10/30

> *"1:36 pm: cardiologist came in for an update. Echo was clear. He is still planning on the TTE procedure most likely tomorrow. Family was under the impression that was no longer happening."*

The institutional miscommunication. The family is finding out about scheduled procedures via the caretaker's hourly note. The hospital is operating on its own logic, separately from the people loving the patient.

This becomes explicit:

> *"It's incredible how many missteps a hospital makes if they're not being constantly supervised and nagged. Today Dad was going in for a surgery at 1pm that was not only not authorized, it was advised against by the neurologist and primary. It was an invasive EKG he would have to be put under for. Luckily, Amy was there to stop it from happening and unwind it all."*

**The hospital as opponent.** Not enemy — opponent. The family is fighting the institution to keep their father from being subjected to procedures he did not agree to and that his other doctors advised against. *Luckily, Amy was there.* The contingency of survival. If Amy had been at lunch.

> *"Additionally, whole foods (spaghetti and meatsauce) have been delivered to the room for my dad to eat even those he's been on a puree diet since day 2."*

The system not seeing him. Multiple times. He is a chart that doesn't update. Spaghetti delivered to a man on puree.

> *"Meanwhile, still no improvement in memory. Dad is not making sense when talking and continues to be forgetful of all conversions in the short term. We keep telling him he needs to eat food and we're leaving to go home tomorrow. He says, 'Alright! Good!' He will forget this conversation happened. He's engaged in that moment though and excited about the optimism. We will continue this each day."*

The cycle made explicit. They tell him a piece of good news. He responds with happiness. He forgets. They tell him again. He responds again. They're making him happy on a loop. **Caretaking by short loop.**

The exclamation point in *Alright! Good!* is one of his exclamation points — paired with the *OK Great!* from page three. The exclamation point as the sound of his old self.

## Page 14 — the Family Letter

> *"Dear Family,"*

The genre shift. Eleven days of timestamped log give way to a formal letter. The voice consolidates. The plural *we* — Annie, Jay, Ken Sanders, and the writer (Amy?) — speaks in paragraphs again.

> *"Initially, the cause of his fall was unclear, but an MRI later revealed he had suffered a major stroke, affecting the back of his brain."*

Now the reveal is given in proper prose. The MRI line that arrived flat in the caretaker log returns here in narrative shape. The same fact, two voices.

> *"This stroke, along with dementia, has impacted his ability to swallow safely, causing him to aspirate when he eats or drinks."*

The swallow test now decoded. The barium test was the test for whether he could continue to be fed at all. He failed it.

> *"A feeding tube would be required to sustain his life, possibly on a permanent basis."*

The word *sustain*. The word *life*. The conditional *would*.

> *"After much discussion and careful review of Dad's healthcare wishes, as well as a heartfelt moment of clarity he had with us, Annie, Jay, Ken Sanders, and I have made the decision to release him into hospice care."*

**A heartfelt moment of clarity.** The piece could circle this phrase forever. He had a moment. They will not describe it. They had a conversation in which he was, for once, fully present, and they decided what came next based on that conversation. The document does not give us the conversation. The conversation belongs to them.

This is one of the document's deepest acts of restraint. **Whatever the moment was, it stays in the family.** The reader is told that it happened and is not told what was said. A line was drawn around it.

> *"He has been discharged from the hospital and moved to a rented home in Newport Beach, where he can spend his final days surrounded by family, love, and comfort."*

*Rented home.* The detail collapses everything. They rented a house for him to die in. There is no family home in California for him to be brought to — they had to rent one. The whole December 2023 plan — the new house in Huntington Beach, the move home — was refused. They had to do it anyway, in compressed form, for his last days.

> *"This home has a beautiful ocean view where he can look out from his hospital bed in the living room, with us gathered around, cooking, playing music, and sharing laughter—just as he would want."*

The composed scene. *Cooking, playing music, sharing laughter.* Italics implied. They are constructing the death they think he should have.

> *"We know how he dislikes sadness and tears."*

**The killer line.** The lifelong refusal of feeling, named one last time, this time as the architecture of the dying. They are not crying. Because *we know how he dislikes sadness and tears.* They are still managing him. They are still arranging themselves around his refusals. The document has been documenting this all along — the man whose bipolar disallowed him from feeling feelings, whose hard exterior closed at any vulnerability — and now they are giving him the death that completes his pattern.

This sentence is the document's most quietly devastating ethical statement. The children meeting their father where he refused to be met. **The piece, if it has a single emotional spine, can run along this sentence.**

> *"We welcome anyone who would like to say goodbye. If you would like to visit, please reach out, and we'll make arrangements."*

The hospitality of the dying.

> *"Thank you to everyone who has reached out; we feel your love, and so does he."*

The closing decorums. *We feel your love and so does he.* He cannot recognize his own children but they are speaking on his behalf. They have absorbed his love-receiving function.

> *"With love and gratitude,*
> *Amy, Annie, and Jay*
> *."*

**The period.**

The period sits on its own line below the names. Probably an artifact of how the doc was formatted. Probably accidental. **Use it anyway.** The period as the document's last gesture. The full stop after the daughter, the daughter, the son.

This is where the document ends. It does not say he died. It does not need to. The hospice decision is the death. The reader supplies the rest.

## Structural observations across the whole document

### The voice shifts

Three voices, mostly distinct:

1. **Jay (primary narrator)** — operational, observant, occasionally lyrical. Has the most named subjectivity. Is the only sibling who handles the worst of the bodily care directly.
2. **Amy/Annie (interspersed)** — group voice. Shows up in the *we* and in the email signature.
3. **Tianna (caretaker)** — flat, professional, devastatingly precise. The vital sign blocks are hers.

The document does not announce the voice shifts. The reader has to feel them. **This is the document's most sophisticated formal device, and it was not engineered.** It happened because multiple people were typing into the same Google Doc.

A piece built from this document could foreground the voice-shift. Could number it. Could color-code it. Could make the shift its own structural device.

### The patterning of recurring phrases

| Recurrence | What it does |
|---|---|
| *Tastes like Heaven / Feels like Heaven* | The chorus. The man's pleasure surviving everything. |
| *Where's Jay?* | The recursive failure. The unrecognizing son. |
| *Dad / client* | The voice marker. The relational vs the institutional. |
| Vital sign blocks | The body's report. Recurring almost-identical. |
| *He fell back asleep / dozed off* | He is mostly absent. |
| *Brief / diaper* | The reversal of care. |

### What the document refuses to do

- It refuses to interpret. It reports.
- It refuses to assign blame. The father's choices are described, not judged. The hospital's failures are noted, not litigated.
- It refuses to grieve openly. The grief is in the syntax — the *bursted into tears*, the *waste-down*, the lowercase *i* — but never in the content.
- It refuses to resolve the central mystery (how long was he down).
- It refuses to describe the moment of clarity.
- It refuses to say he died.

**These refusals are the spaces where your voice can enter.** Not to fill them — to honor them by doing something different in the negative space.
